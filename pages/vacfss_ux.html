VACFSS UX Research

Uncovering why potential caregivers couldn't find the most important page on the site, and what happened after we fixed it.

At a Glance

Problem: Potential caregivers were arriving from ads, but the organization's most important page was buried. Even staff who used the site regularly needed over 2 minutes to find it.

What I did: Interviewed staff, reviewed how past caregivers first made contact, and turned the findings into three fixes: a more direct path to the page, scannable content, and real community photography.

Outcome: Caregiver inquiries rose 566% in the 12 months after the March 2024 page and ad changes. Inquiries from sources other than Facebook rose 134%.

Validation: Follow-up caregiver interviews in 2026 confirmed that the direct ad path removed the navigation barrier and that the more descriptive content helped them take the next step.

Discovery

Background

VACFSS (Vancouver Aboriginal Child and Family Services Society) supports Indigenous children and families in Vancouver. A critical part of their work is recruiting foster and family caregivers — yet the website's path to that information was buried, text-heavy, and filled with stock photography that looked identical to every other social services organization.

I conducted two rounds of research, staff interviews in 2024 and caregiver interviews in 2026, to measure the impact of navigation and content changes on the site's core goal: getting a potential caregiver to reach out.

Research

Round 1: Staff Interviews (2024)

I started by testing with 5 VACFSS staff members (2 communications employees, 2 social workers, and 1 caregiver), people who use the site regularly. The core research question was whether even internal users could efficiently navigate to the organization's most important conversion page.

"Do you know which is our Becoming a Caregiver page?" — Interview prompt given to 5 staff participants
!

Critical Finding: 2+ Minutes to Navigate

Every participant took over 2 minutes to locate the Becoming a Caregiver page, despite working at the organization. The cause was the site structure. If staff couldn't find it quickly, a potential caregiver arriving from an ad had almost no chance.

Supporting Data: Referral Pattern (2024)

3 out of 5 past caregivers were directly referred by someone they knew, so they never needed to navigate the site. Word of mouth was carrying recruitment.

Referrals hid the navigation problem because those people already knew where to call.

Pain Points Identified

1

Navigation Depth to a Critical Page

The Becoming a Caregiver page was buried several levels deep with no prominent calls to action on the homepage or top-level navigation. For someone arriving cold from a Facebook ad, the path was invisible.

2

Lengthy, Unbroken Blocks of Text

The site relied on dense paragraphs with no headers, callouts, or scannable structure, so potential caregivers couldn't quickly find answers.

3

Stock Photography That Erased Identity

The site's generic stock images looked like those of dozens of other social services organizations. They did not represent an Indigenous-led organization whose mission centres on community connection.

Design

Before & After: The Caregiver Page

The biggest improvement was restructuring the Becoming a Caregiver page: shorter text blocks, clear visual hierarchy, and a prominent call to action.

Validation

Round 2: Caregiver Interviews (2026)

In 2026, I followed up with caregivers who had come through the Facebook recruitment pipeline to understand their actual experience navigating from ad to inquiry.

"Was it easy to find the Becoming a Caregiver page?" — Follow-up interview question, 2026

Finding: Direct Ad Path Removed the Navigation Problem

Of the caregivers interviewed in 2026, 3 had come directly through the Facebook page. When asked about finding the caregiver page, they said the process felt simple because the ad linked directly to it, removing the navigation barrier entirely.

They said the more descriptive content on the redesigned page answered their questions and gave them enough context to take the next step.

Both fixes worked: the ad reduced navigation friction, and the page content turned interest into action.

Takeaways

Key Takeaways

Staff who knew the site still needed 2+ minutes, so the problem was the site structure. The fix had two parts: make the page discoverable (direct ad links and improved navigation) and make it worth staying on (restructured content and on-site photography).

Inquiries rose 566% in the 12 months after the changes, and 134% from sources other than Facebook, which suggests the page and the ads worked together.

On-site photography shows the real community of an Indigenous-led organization, which builds the trust someone needs before becoming a caregiver.

Limits of this evidence: Both interview rounds were small, starting with 5 staff in 2024, and the ad path and page content changed together. The next step would be task-based testing with people who have never seen the site.

Production Process

1

Market Research

I researched "day in the life" recruitment videos across social services, healthcare, and non-profit sectors. Most failed because they felt scripted or promotional, so I set the benchmark at showing the real work.

2

Equipment Decisions

Based on the research, I bought equipment for a handheld, documentary-style look.

3

Co-Direction & Scripting

I co-directed and scripted the video with VACFSS staff. The script mapped the questions a prospective social worker would have, then answered them with real footage.

4

Filming & Editing

I filmed and edited the video, ending on the emotional case for the role.

How It Was Used

The video was screened during in-person recruitment visits to social work colleges to preview the role day-to-day. Students who attended could share it, and students who didn't could still watch it.
