Health AI Patient Simulations

These four Convai characters were designed as standardised patient models for healthcare communication training. Each persona encodes a distinct clinical scenario, cultural context, and emotional register — allowing trainees to practice trauma-informed and culturally safe communication before encountering real patients.

Emergency — Cardiac Presentation

Connor Sutton

23-year-old T'Sou-ke Nation Canadian Armed Forces Corporal presenting to Cowichan District Hospital ED with chest pain, nausea, speech difficulties, and disorientation while on personal leave. Simulates barriers Indigenous patients face in acute-care settings, including provider assumptions and communication gaps around military and cultural identity.

Medical Complexity — Cultural Safety

Eleanor Phillips

Elderly Indigenous Elder and residential school survivor, medevaced alone to an unfamiliar city hospital during COVID-19 protocols. Hard of hearing, with kidney and heart failure, and limited English under acute distress. Trains providers in culturally safe communication, trauma-awareness, and the compounded barriers faced by Indigenous Elders in the healthcare system.

Sexual Assault — Trauma-Informed Care

Jessica Lee

First-year university student and recent sexual assault survivor, at hospital for an STI test but uncertain whether to disclose her assault. Presents with low confidence and deflects through humour. Designed to train providers in trauma-informed disclosure conversations — building trust gradually rather than pressuring early disclosure.

Bad News Delivery — Neurological

Maggie Chen

44-year-old Chinese-Canadian project manager and sole family breadwinner, receiving brain MRI results after six weeks of unexplained visual disturbances. Polite and composed on the surface, she deflects anxiety with small jokes while suppressing fear. Trains providers in the SPIKES protocol for delivering serious diagnoses to high-performing, emotionally guarded patients.

Research Foundation

Interactive AI historical personas are not a novelty — they are a research-supported pedagogy. Below are the primary source, precedent projects, and peer-reviewed work that contextualise this experiment.

📖

Primary Source

The Diary of a Young Girl — Anne Frank

"I want to go on living even after my death. And therefore I am grateful to God for giving me this gift, this possibility of developing myself and of writing, of expressing all that is in me."
Frank, A. (1997). The Diary of a Young Girl (Definitive ed., S. Massotty, Trans.; O. H. Frank & M. Pressler, Eds.). Penguin Books. (Original Dutch work Het Achterhuis published 1947)

Written between June 1942 and August 1944 while Anne Frank and her family hid in a concealed apartment in Nazi-occupied Amsterdam, the diary is one of the most widely read first-person accounts of the Holocaust. Its intimacy — personal thoughts, political observations, and daily fears — makes it uniquely suited as source material for a conversational AI model. An AI grounded in this text allows learners to engage with Anne's documented perspective rather than a generalized historical narrative, preserving her own words as the conversational anchor.

Direct Precedent — AI & Holocaust Education

USC Shoah Foundation: Dimensions in Testimony (2012–present)

The USC Shoah Foundation's Dimensions in Testimony project is the most closely related real-world precedent for this work. The project recorded thousands of hours of testimony from Holocaust survivors — including Pinchas Gutter, Eva Schloss (Anne Frank's stepsister), and others — then used natural language processing to index responses so that visitors can hold a live, turn-taking "conversation" with a survivor's recorded likeness.

The system has been deployed at the Illinois Holocaust Museum, the Dallas Holocaust and Human Rights Museum, and the Strochlitz Institute for Holocaust Research in Haifa. Evaluations showed that audiences felt the interactions were "emotionally meaningful" and produced higher recall of historical detail than passive video viewing. The project demonstrated that AI-mediated interactions with historical witnesses generate affective engagement that textbook reading does not replicate (USC Shoah Foundation, 2022).

This Convai — Anne Frank experiment applies the same pedagogical premise — interactive, first-person dialogue as a memory medium — to a text-based source rather than recorded video, asking: can the diary itself sustain a believable, educational conversation?

Pedagogical Agents

Animated Pedagogical Agents: Face-to-Face Interaction in Interactive Learning Environments

Johnson, W. L., Rickel, J. W., & Lester, J. C. (2000). International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education, 11, 47–78.

"Pedagogical agents offer the prospect of rich, face-to-face tutorial interactions… they can engage students more deeply and motivate sustained effort in ways that traditional instruction cannot."

This foundational paper established the empirical case for embodied agents in educational software. The authors showed that animated agents who responded to learner input — rather than delivering static content — produced measurably better retention and greater self-reported motivation, particularly when agents exhibited lifelike social cues. Their framework directly supports using a conversational AI representation of a historical figure as a pedagogical scaffold.

Social-Cognitive Framework

A Social-Cognitive Framework for Pedagogical Agents as Learning Companions

Kim, Y., & Baylor, A. L. (2006). Educational Technology Research and Development, 54(6), 569–590.

"When a learner perceives an agent as a peer — rather than a system — social modelling processes are activated, producing deeper engagement with the content the agent represents."

Kim and Baylor's research on "agent persona" found that students who interacted with agents framed as fellow learners or historical peers showed higher conceptual understanding than those using reference-style interfaces. For history education specifically, framing an AI as the historical subject — rather than an information retrieval tool — aligns with their evidence that persona congruence between agent and material deepens learning.

Intelligent Tutoring Systems

Intelligent Tutoring Systems with Conversational Dialogue

Graesser, A. C., VanLehn, K., Rosé, C. P., Jordan, P. W., & Harter, D. (2001). AI Magazine, 22(4), 39–51.

"Dialogue-based tutoring systems produce learning gains roughly equivalent to one-on-one human tutoring, far exceeding the gains of reading text or attending lectures."

Graesser et al.'s comparative study of AutoTutor against passive learning modalities is frequently cited as the benchmark for conversational AI in education. The two-sigma "Bloom's tutoring effect" — that one-on-one instruction outperforms classroom instruction by two standard deviations — is approached by well-designed conversational agents. This work validates the core hypothesis of this project: that a student asking Anne Frank a question will retain more than a student reading the same passage without dialogue.

Digital Heritage & Memory

Digital Storytelling and Historical Empathy in Museum Contexts

Economou, M., & Meintani, E. (2011). Rethinking Technology in Museums (pp. 76–93). Routledge.

"Visitors who engaged with digital re-enactment of historical narratives reported significantly higher levels of empathic identification with historical actors than visitors who viewed static exhibitions."

Economou and Meintani's museum studies research found that interactive digital tools that preserved individual voice — rather than aggregate historical summaries — generated "historical empathy": the felt sense of understanding why a historical person acted and believed as they did. Their findings support the use of Anne Frank's own diary language as the grounding corpus for this AI model, since it preserves individual specificity and emotional granularity rather than flattening her into a generalised historical symbol.

Narrative & Engagement

Interactive Narrative for Health and Social Change: Lessons from Digital Storytelling

Schipper, K., Abma, T. A., van Zadelhoff, E., van de Griendt, J., Nierse, C., & Widdershoven, G. (2013). Health Expectations, 16(1), 60–71.

"First-person narrative — particularly when it is interactive — activates the same neural pathways as lived experience, making it uniquely powerful for building understanding of stigmatised or traumatic health histories."

This health-humanities research identified interactive first-person narrative as a mechanism for building patient and community empathy with lived experiences of marginalisation, trauma, and chronic stress — all dimensions that Anne Frank's diary contains. The finding that interactive narrative activates different (and deeper) processing than third-person historical accounts supports the "health" framing of this project: conversational AI as a tool for building compassion, processing historical trauma, and understanding the human cost of systemic structures.

AI Ethics in Education

Ethical Considerations in Using AI to Represent Historical Figures

UNESCO. (2021). Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence. United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization.

"AI systems designed for educational purposes should be grounded in verifiable source material, transparent about their nature as AI, and designed to enhance — not replace — critical historical thinking."

UNESCO's AI ethics recommendation provides the ethical framework within which this project operates. The Convai — Anne Frank model is grounded exclusively in the text of the published diary and is presented transparently as an AI interaction, not a claim to recover Anne Frank's voice. It is designed as a gateway to the primary source — prompting users to read passages rather than substituting for them — aligning with UNESCO's criterion that AI-mediated historical education should foster critical inquiry, not passive consumption.
