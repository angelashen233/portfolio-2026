Convai — Health AI Patient Simulations

Four AI-powered standardised patient characters built on the Convai platform for healthcare communication training. Each persona encodes a distinct clinical scenario, cultural context, and emotional register — letting trainees practice trauma-informed and culturally safe communication before encountering real patients.

This work is part of a broader Convai character study that also includes a historical education prototype — Convai — Anne Frank ↗ — which explores how AI personas grounded in primary sources can make personal history emotionally vivid for learners.

Patient Simulation Characters

Each character was built using Convai's Core Description, Speaking Style, and Personality configuration. They are designed to reward empathetic, structured communication and surface the consequences of rushed or culturally unsafe approaches.

Emergency — Cardiac Presentation

Connor Sutton

23-year-old T'Sou-ke Nation Canadian Armed Forces Corporal presenting to Cowichan District Hospital ED with chest pain, nausea, speech difficulties, and disorientation while on personal leave. Simulates barriers Indigenous patients face in acute-care settings, including provider assumptions and communication gaps around military and cultural identity.

Medical Complexity — Cultural Safety

Eleanor Phillips

Elderly Indigenous Elder and residential school survivor, medevaced alone to an unfamiliar city hospital during COVID-19 protocols. Hard of hearing, with kidney and heart failure, and limited English under acute distress. Trains providers in culturally safe communication, trauma-awareness, and the compounded barriers faced by Indigenous Elders in the healthcare system.

Sexual Assault — Trauma-Informed Care

Jessica Lee

First-year university student and recent sexual assault survivor, at hospital for an STI test but uncertain whether to disclose her assault. Presents with low confidence and deflects through humour. Designed to train providers in trauma-informed disclosure conversations — building trust gradually rather than pressuring early disclosure.

Bad News Delivery — Neurological

Maggie Chen

44-year-old Chinese-Canadian project manager and sole family breadwinner, receiving brain MRI results after six weeks of unexplained visual disturbances. Polite and composed on the surface, she deflects anxiety with small jokes while suppressing fear. Trains providers in the SPIKES protocol for delivering serious diagnoses to high-performing, emotionally guarded patients.

Clinical Training Demo — Bad News Delivery

Maggie Chen — Delivering Difficult Diagnoses (SPIKES Protocol)

This demo is grounded in UBC's medical communication curriculum. UBC's Faculty of Medicine formally integrates bad-news delivery training using the SPIKES protocol, which is hosted and disseminated by UBC Continuing Professional Development (ubccpd.ca). The British Columbia Medical Journal has confirmed SPIKES training is included in UBC's undergraduate medical curriculum. UBC's internal medicine residency program (115+ residents) supplements it with Academic Half Days and standardised patient (SP) role-play. This Convai character serves as an AI-powered SP for that final step.

Maggie Chen — a 44-year-old Chinese-Canadian project manager and sole family breadwinner — is being told results of a brain MRI suggesting a small meningioma near her optic nerve. She deflects anxiety with polite humour; her fear surfaces only when the physician creates genuine safety. The student-doctor is evaluated against the six SPIKES steps: Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Emotions, and Strategy/Summary.

⚠ Technical note — current prototype limitations:
The Convai platform does not yet animate facial expressions or body gestures for this character; the avatar holds a neutral, static pose. To compensate, the model has been prompted to verbalise physical and emotional cues that would normally be conveyed through expression — for example, announcing "You can see my hands are shaking" or "I feel a tightening in my chest." This verbal description approach maintains emotional realism without requiring avatar animation. Additionally, verbal response lag (latency between student input and character reply) is present throughout — however, the first ~3 minutes of interaction function at acceptable quality, making short structured exchanges workable for early-stage SPIKES training.

Clinical Training Demo — Emergency / Indigenous Patient Experience

Connor Sutton — Barriers in the Emergency Department

Connor Sutton's case is directly rooted in the kind of incident documented in the In Plain Sight report (Turpel-Lafond, 2020) — BC's provincial review of Indigenous-specific racism in health care. The report included first-person accounts of Indigenous patients presenting to emergency departments with acute symptoms and being directed to homeless shelters or otherwise turned away rather than assessed and admitted for overnight observation. Sutton, a 23-year-old T'Sou-ke Nation Corporal presenting with chest pain, nausea, and speech difficulties, is a composite character trained to surface exactly this dynamic: a clinically serious presentation meets provider assumptions that override proper triage.

In the simulation, trainees must recognise and counteract the implicit bias that led to Sutton being told — in the real cases the report documents — to seek a shelter rather than receive an ED bed for symptom monitoring overnight. The character is designed so that a dismissive or rushed interaction compounds his reluctance to advocate for himself, while a trauma-informed, culturally safe approach opens a fuller clinical picture.

⚠ Technical note — short-response prototype:
This recording captures early-stage testing where the character produces short, clipped responses. Verbal lag is present and the response depth is limited compared to longer free-form exchanges. The clip demonstrates the interaction loop and triage-scenario framing rather than a fully developed dialogue flow.
